## c.	How to talk with children as design partners

### i. Establishing equality
Co-design asks adults and children to work as partners. The way you talk with children can support that partnership or undercut it.

1. Remember that you start with the advantage. Adults have more power, influence and knowledge than children, and that imbalance does not go away on its own (Yip et al., 2017).
1. Tell children that they are not being tested and that there are no right or wrong answers (Kendrick, 2019).
1. Show that you are interested. A blank "poker face" makes children under 5 quieter and less responsive. Use praise that does not steer them, such as "all of this is really helpful to see" (Kendrick, 2019).
1. Balance is fundamental to the design partner relationship. It is least balanced when adults and children don't exchange and mix ideas, for example when adults only ask questions and write down the answers (Yip et al., 2017).

### ii. Age appropriate language

#### Younger children (about ages 4 to 7)

1. Say exactly what you mean. Children take words literally: in one study, children who were asked whether they had been on a "school field trip" answered no, because they had been on a "class field trip" (Holaday & Turner-Henson, 1989, as cited in Borgers et al., 2000).
1. Use the child's own words. Listen for the word a child uses for something, then use that same word when you ask about it (Borgers et al., 2000).
1. Do not hint at the answer you want. Children of this age are very suggestible (Borgers et al., 2000). Ask "What do you think of this one?" instead of "You like this one, don't you?"

#### Older children (about ages 8 to 11)

1. Phrase questions positively. Children aged 8 to 11 still read words literally and have trouble with negations (Borgers et al., 2000). For example, a negative statement with a tag:
    * Avoid: "You didn't get stuck anywhere, did you?"
    * Ask: "Did you get stuck anywhere?"

    The first also hints at the answer you want, so it doubles as an example of steering.
1. Avoid vague words such as "sometimes" or "often". Children of this age do not cope with questions that can be read in more than one way (Borgers et al., 2000; de Leeuw, 2011). A yes-or-no question about something they did works better (de Leeuw, 2011).
1. Use everyday words in place of adult ones. Simpler Common words work better. For example,changing "transition" to "switch" (Lampman & Lawlor, n.d.).

| Using the child's own word for it: "Tell me about your class field trip." | "Did you go on a school field trip?" | Tell me about what you made. | Is that a robot? | <a href="https://www.epicentro.iss.it/okkioallasalute/documenti/letteratura_scientifica/Borghers_questionnaires%20children.pdf">Borgers et al. (2000)</a> |

#### Example B: Quiet child

| Good | Bad | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Use an open-ended question: "What would you change about this?" | "Do you have anything to add?" A closed question gets a one-word answer. | <a href="https://eurochild.org/uploads/2021/01/We_Are_Here_Toolbox.pdf">Pinto et al. (2020, p. 120)</a> |
| Giving 2 to 5 minutes of quiet thinking or pair talk first, then asking: "What did the two of you come up with?" | Asking the whole group to call out ideas to the room. | <a href="https://eurochild.org/uploads/2021/01/We_Are_Here_Toolbox.pdf">Pinto et al. (2020, p. 120)</a> |
| Giving the child a printed sheet of emoji to point at to start talking about how it felt. | Accepting "it was fine" from a child who frowned and sighed all the way through. | <a href="https://www.bentley.edu/centers/user-experience-center/usabilitytestingwithminors">Lampman and Lawlor (n.d.)</a> |

#### Example C: Older child (ages 7 to 11)

| Good | Bad | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Questioning the idea and building on it: "How would that help someone using it? Oh, so it turns it into a kind of game. What if we added…" | Only asking for likes, dislikes and ideas and writing them down, adding none of your own. | <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025787">Yip et al. (2017)</a> |
| "Which part should I work on? Show me how you want it." | Watching children build and only pointing at parts to ask what they are. | <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025787">Yip et al. (2017)</a> |
| Offering options and letting the child decide: "We could tape it to the wall, or I could hold it up. Which do you want?" | "You need to pick one of these two." A child who likes neither may just stop. | <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025787">Yip et al. (2017)</a> |
